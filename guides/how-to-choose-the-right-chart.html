How to choose the right chart

Most bad charts aren't ugly. They answer the wrong question. Here's a framework that starts from the question and ends with a chart people understand in seconds.

Data.Studio Editorial · Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Step 1: Write the question down

Before opening any tool, finish this sentence: "After seeing this chart, my reader should know that…". "Revenue grew every month except April" is a trend question. "The North region sells twice as much as the South" is a comparison. "Most orders are under $50, but a few are over $1,000" is a distribution question. The sentence decides the chart. The data alone doesn't.

If you can't finish the sentence, you're still exploring. That's fine, but exploratory charts for yourself and explanatory charts for an audience are different jobs. Explore freely, then rebuild the one chart that carries the message.

Step 3: Respect how eyes read charts

Decades of perception research point to a consistent ranking. People judge position along a common scale most accurately, then length, then angle and area, and finally colour intensity. That explains a few durable rules:

  • Bars beat pies for anything beyond a rough share, because comparing bar lengths is easier than comparing slice angles.
  • Bar charts start at zero. The bar's length is the encoding, so a truncated axis exaggerates differences. Line charts can zoom in, because their encoding is position and slope.
  • Bubble sizes mislead. Doubling a value should double a bubble's area, not its diameter. Even when done right, readers underestimate area differences.
  • Colour is for categories or emphasis, not for precise values. Use one highlight colour for the thing you're talking about and grey for context.

Step 4: Design for the reader, not the dataset

Sort with purpose

Unless categories have a natural order (months, age bands, survey scales), sort bars by value. A sorted chart turns a lookup task into a ranking the eye reads instantly.

Label directly

Legends make readers bounce between key and chart. Place series names at the end of lines and values on or beside bars where space allows.

Title with the finding

"Monthly revenue, 2026" describes the axes. "Revenue grew 47% in six months, led by the app" tells the story. The second gets remembered.

Cut what doesn't carry information

Heavy gridlines, 3D effects, drop shadows and decorative backgrounds compete with the data. Remove them, then check whether anything important disappeared. Usually nothing did.

Common mistakes and quick fixes

  • Too many lines ("spaghetti chart"). Highlight one or two series and grey out the rest, or use small multiples, one mini chart per series on shared axes.
  • Pie with 12 slices. Switch to a sorted horizontal bar chart, or group small slices into "Other".
  • Dual y-axes. Two scales on one chart invite false correlations. Use two charts stacked vertically with a shared x-axis.
  • Counts on a map. Population drives raw counts. Map rates per capita instead.
  • Uneven time intervals drawn evenly spaced. Make sure the axis reflects real time gaps.

Accessibility checklist

  • Don't rely on colour alone. Add labels, patterns or direct annotations.
  • Choose palettes distinguishable with common colour-vision deficiencies (avoid red/green as the only contrast).
  • Keep text at least 12px and maintain good contrast against the background.
  • Provide a one-sentence text summary of the key finding near the chart.
